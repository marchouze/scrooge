## 1. Why this gate matters — three-letter false-positive risk

A three-letter string carries a structurally elevated false-positive risk in language-clearance:

- **Mathematical.** A three-letter string "Hoz" has at most 26³ = 17,576 possible forms in the Latin alphabet; the number of three-letter "real words" across the world's natural languages is non-trivially dense within that space. Any specific three-letter form has a higher base probability of colliding with a real word in *some* language than a four- or five-letter form does.
- **Phonetic.** Tonal languages (Mandarin) and click-consonant languages (isiXhosa, isiZulu) carry phonetic-collision risk that the written form does not surface.
- **Slang / register.** Native speakers detect slang, regional usage, offensive register, or unintended associations that dictionary-only scans miss.
- **Compound / homophone.** A three-letter form can be a recognisable component of a longer compound word, an abbreviation, or a homophone of a different word in another language.

The Constitution requires the bank to operate without giving offence to or excluding any of the eleven official languages; the institutional-counterparty profile additionally requires no offence / unintended meaning in the institutional-international set.

## 3. Scope — substantive checks per language

For each language in scope, the formal sweep must check:

- **Dictionary-form meaning.** Does "Hoz" exist as a word in the language? If yes, what does it mean?
- **Phonetic neighbour.** Does "Hoz" sound like (or transliterate to) a word with a meaning that creates an issue? (Especially relevant for tonal / click-consonant / non-Latin-script languages.)
- **Slang / register.** Native speakers check for slang, regional, or register-specific usage.
- **Offensive / sensitive connotation.** Native speakers check for any politically, religiously, culturally, or commercially sensitive association.
- **Brand association.** Native speakers check for any existing brand, trademark, or commercial entity in that language-market that uses "Hoz" or a near-form.
- **Pronunciation / readability.** How does "Hoz" pronounce in that language? Is it readable / pronounceable to a native speaker without prompt?

## 4. Self-administered preliminary check

Imani has run a dictionary-level scan across publicly-accessible language resources for the eleven SA official languages and the institutional-international set.

`[preliminary: dictionary-scan-only]`

### 4.1 Eleven SA official languages — dictionary-level findings

**No common-word collision identified at the dictionary level for "Hoz" in any of the eleven SA languages.**

Caveats (all three apply across all eleven):

- (a) This is a dictionary-only scan; it does not detect slang, register, regional usage, or offensive connotation.
- (b) It does not detect homophones or near-homophones in tonal or click-consonant languages where written form alone misses the spoken form.
- (c) Spelling-variation and orthographic-variation in some languages (e.g. older orthographies of isiZulu / isiXhosa) may produce dictionary-level forms that a Latin-alphabet Hoz scan does not surface.

**PAX must validate with native speakers.** The dictionary scan is necessary but not sufficient.

### 4.2 Institutional-international set — dictionary-level findings

`[preliminary: dictionary-scan-only]`

- **Spanish.** "Hoz" is a recognisable Spanish word — meaning "sickle" or "scythe", and also a place-name (e.g. Hoz de Anero in Cantabria). Public-domain awareness only. Whether this creates a brand-association or unintended-meaning issue in the institutional-counterparty Spanish-speaking footprint (LatAm + Spain) is for PAX + native-speaker analysis.
- **Polish.** "Hoz" is not standard Polish but is a recognisable surname / placename in some Central European usage; public-domain awareness only.
- **Hungarian.** Similar — recognisable as part of compound words but not a standalone common-meaning form; public-domain awareness only.
- **French / Portuguese / Mandarin / Arabic / Russian.** No common-word collision identified at dictionary-level. Caveats per § 4.1 apply.

### 4.3 Surfacing for PAX

The Spanish-language finding is the single most material surfacing for PAX's institutional-international leg. The "sickle / scythe" meaning is not inherently offensive but is a very specific concrete object, which can read as an unintended brand association in a financial-services context. PAX's Spanish native-speaker check should explicitly opine on whether the brand association creates an issue or is sufficiently abstract to clear.
